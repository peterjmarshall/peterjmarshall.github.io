Peter J. Marshall

I am a Professor of Psychology and Neuroscience at Temple University. My research has spanned various topics in developmental psychology and developmental neuroscience.

I have also become increasingly interested in broader questions about development: how new forms and capacities arise, and how we can explain developmental change without assuming a predetermined outcome.

This site brings together my research, open educational resources, and related writing.

About

I trained in zoology and developmental psychology at the University of Cambridge. My doctoral and postdoctoral work focused on temperament, attachment, early experience, and aspects of brain development. I spent several years at the University of Maryland before coming to Temple in 2004.

Research

Since coming to Temple University, much of my work has explored the connections among perception and action, bodily experience, and relations between self and other, with a particular focus on infants and young children. More recently, that work has extended into the development of neural body maps and their relation to behavioral development. A second line of research examines how children interact with and learn from new technologies, including social robots and artificial intelligence.

The Developmental Science Lab site has information about current research, people, participation, and our work with infants and families.

Open Educational Resources

I have been developing two open educational resources for students and other interested readers. They begin from different questions, but they increasingly intersect.

About Development

Development is often explained by listing the things that influence it: genes, environments, experience, culture. About Development starts with a different question: how do organized forms actually arise, recur, and change? It draws on developmental science, biology, neuroscience, and the history of ideas about development.

Visit About Development →

Enacting Life

Enacting Life introduces a biologically-oriented appraoch to the study of mind. It explores how living organisms maintain themselves, act in relation to their environments, and make sense of the world, and what this means for how we think about cognition, brains, development, and artificial intelligence.
