Peter J. Marshall

I am a developmental psychologist with a background in biology and neuroscience. Most of my empirical work has examined developing brains and bodies, using EEG and, more recently, naturalistic video and computer vision.

Alongside that work, I have kept returning to a broader problem: what exactly are we trying to explain when we explain development? I am interested in how organized forms and capacities take shape over time, how brains develop as parts of living bodies, and what changes when we take development itself seriously rather than treating it mainly as the route to an already specified outcome.

The materials here are different ways of working on those questions.

Open Educational Resources

I have been developing two open educational resources for students and other interested readers. They overlap, but they begin from different questions.

About Development

Development is often explained by listing the things that influence it: genes, environments, experience, culture. About Development starts with a different question: how do organized forms actually arise, recur, and change? It draws on developmental science, biology, neuroscience, and the history of ideas about development.

Visit About Development →

Enacting Life

Enacting Life begins with a simple but difficult question: what is different about a living thing? It follows one biological strand of enactivism into questions about cognition, the brain, meaning, and artificial intelligence.

About

I trained in zoology and developmental psychology at the University of Cambridge, then spent several years at the University of Maryland before coming to Temple in 2004. I spent 2014–15 as a visiting scholar at the Institute for Learning & Brain Sciences at the University of Washington.

My research has moved through temperament and early experience, developmental psychophysiology, action and imitation, infant body representations, anticipation, and the development of reaching and grasping. The topics have changed over the years, but questions about how biological and psychological organization develops have kept resurfacing.

I am Professor of Psychology and Neuroscience at Temple University.
