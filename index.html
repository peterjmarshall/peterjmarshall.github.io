Peter J. Marshall

I am a developmental psychologist with a background in biology and neuroscience. Much of my research has involved the developing brain, particularly the ways that bodily activity, experience, and social interaction become part of development.

Alongside that empirical work, I have kept returning to a broader problem: what exactly are we trying to explain when we explain development? I am interested in how organized forms and capacities take shape over time, how brains develop as parts of living bodies, and what changes when we take development itself seriously rather than treating it mainly as the route to an already specified outcome.

The projects collected here are different ways of working on those questions.

Open Educational Resources

I have been developing two open educational resources for students and other interested readers. They overlap, but they begin from different questions.

About Development

Development is often explained by listing the things that influence it: genes, environments, experience, culture. About Development starts with a different question: how do organized forms actually arise, recur, and change? It draws on developmental science, biology, neuroscience, and the history of ideas about development.

Visit About Development →

Enacting Life

What is different about a living system? Enacting Life explores an approach that begins with organisms as active, self-maintaining systems rather than passive receivers of information, and follows that idea into questions about cognition, perception, the brain, meaning, and artificial intelligence.

About

I am Professor of Psychology and Neuroscience at Temple University. My research has moved through several areas of developmental science, including temperament, early experience, developmental psychophysiology, action and imitation, infant body representations, anticipation, and the development of reaching and grasping.

Those projects have often involved EEG and other ways of studying the developing brain, but I have also been increasingly interested in the assumptions that sit behind developmental neuroscience itself. In particular, I am interested in what becomes visible when we treat brains as developing parts of living bodies, and development as a historical process through which new forms of organization take shape.
