About

I studied Natural Sciences at the University of Cambridge, specializing in Zoology, and went on to complete a Ph.D. there with a research focus in developmental psychophysiology. In 1997 I joined the Department of Human Development at the University of Maryland, first as a postdoctoral researcher and later as a Research Assistant Professor. My work there focused on temperament, attachment, early experience and brain development, including the use of EEG to study brain activity in infants and young children.

Towards the end of my time in Maryland, I became interested in a particular rhythm in the EEG signal, the sensorimotor mu rhythm, which led me to become more interested in the connections between perception and action. I followed this line of research when I came to Temple. With students and collaborators, I have examined how carrying out an action changes the brain's response to watching someone else carry out a similar action. A second line of work has looked at anticipation: how sensorimotor rhythms change as children and adults prepare for an expected touch, and how those changes relate to attention and self-control. We have also developed a related line of studies on self–other correspondence in infancy and the development of body maps in the infant brain.

My current research has extended those studies to address connected questions about the relations between behavioral development and brain development. Recent work has examined how infants' emerging abilities to reach for and grasp objects relate to how distinct EEG responses to touch on different fingers are from one another. Through this work, I've become interested in new methods for quantifying hand movements in infants, and in how these data can potentially inform questions about the organization of behavior in infancy and beyond.

Over the years, my work on the relations among action, perception and cognition has drawn me towards theories of embodiment. My interests and writing in this area have concerned how psychology and neuroscience relate to each other and more recently, the continuity of mind and life.

Open Resources

I have put together two freely available online resources, both growing out of my teaching and writing.

About Development

About Development is a set of readings on what development is and how it works. The central question is why development reliably occurs when there is no plan for the finished organism present at earlier stages. It introduces a broad approach to thinking about development that is meant to be of interest to anyone who wants to go beyond the description of "what happens and when" in development.

Enacting Life

Enacting Life is a companion set of readings on a particular way of thinking that began with the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela in the 1970s and has been carried on by others. The starting point for this thread of "enactivism" is that living things are not simply acted upon by the world; they already actively maintain themselves. The readings get further into the notion of embodiment and shed light on the role of neuroscience in psychological explanation. A key theme is what makes living things different from nonliving things, particularly machines. The resource ends with a coda on AI and living sense-making.
