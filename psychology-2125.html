How the Article and This Page Came About

In summer 2025, the APS journal Perspectives on Psychological Science put out a call for papers for a special issue on what psychological science might look like a century from now. The call asked for “bold, methodologically grounded visions” of the discipline. The issue was meant to serve as “a time capsule, a provocation, and a map.”

The task was clearly a challenging one: a century is a long time. Just look at what was going on in 1925 compared with now. But I sent in a proposal and a few weeks later was invited to write a full paper. It took a while for me to focus the message, but after helpful feedback from reviewers the manuscript was accepted as Psychology in 2125: A Developmental Science of Living Sense-Making.

Hopefully the article is clear enough on its own. But the special issue had a 5,000-word limit, which doesn’t allow space to work through much of the background or the finer points of the argument. I started writing this page to provide more context and further articulate why I think the forecast matters. It has become more than I bargained for, and this page is now longer than the article itself. But I hope the fuller discussion makes it easier to see how I arrived at the forecast and what it would mean for psychology.

After a brief outline of the overall argument, I go through the intellectual history behind it and the view of embodiment I draw on. I then work through the argument in more detail, before turning to its stakes and implications. The page closes with a statement of how I used AI in the work.

What Is the Argument?

In the article I argue that psychology in 2125 will have become a “developmental science of living sense-making.” By this I mean a psychology that explains how a person’s ways of engaging with the world develop, and how things come to matter to them through that engagement. My starting concern is that much of psychology treats the significance of a given situation as already being settled, without asking how that situation relates to the person’s history and ongoing activity.

I point out pressures that may lead psychology to change, beginning with the observation that longstanding critiques of genetic blueprints have gained further support from work in developmental and evolutionary biology. The notion of a genetic blueprint might seem removed from the point about “the significance of a given situation”, but it captures the same issue, which is the assumption that information with a pregiven meaning is present in advance.

Another pressure comes from methods for studying change within individuals, which are becoming more widely used. A pattern found across a group does not necessarily describe what happens within any particular person. This is not a new observation, but methods that tap into how a person’s activity holds together and how it changes with their circumstances are becoming more widely accessible and used.

After describing those two pressures, I bring in the way that AI is challenging psychology. Large language models can produce fluent, appropriate responses without appearing to have anything at stake in what they say, which raises the question of what we can infer from behavioral performance alone. But although a machine can now carry out many psychological tasks, how it performs on those tasks means nothing to it. So we can ask what AI tells us about the living person for whom things matter.

In the psychology I forecast, development would be central to explanation. This does not mean making psychology primarily about children. It means asking how a person’s ways of living come about, what matters to them, and what sustains or changes them throughout a life.

Many of the individual points made in the article are not new. Developmental systems theory and process-relational developmental science already offer accounts of how new forms of organization come about and are sustained. Sociocultural approaches show how participation in a cultural world is part of development, while embodied and enactive approaches connect meaning with living activity. These are established traditions, and I am not claiming that the ideas in the forecast are novel. Overton and Palermo’s 1994 edited volume The Nature and Ontogenesis of Meaning, for example, explored meaning-making within developmental psychology. What I am suggesting is that ideas of this kind might become more central to psychology as a whole. The context of the special issue allowed me to speculate that pressures faced by the discipline might in fact force such a shift.

Whether what I forecast will actually happen is another matter, as is what would be done with such a psychology if it does. I return to both questions at the end of the page.

Why I Came to This Argument

My undergraduate degree was in Natural Sciences at the University of Cambridge, where I specialized in Zoology. I stayed on at Cambridge to complete a Ph.D. with Joan Stevenson-Hinde at the Sub-Department of Animal Behaviour, where I worked on a longitudinal study of wariness of the unfamiliar in young children, including how temperamental tendencies relate to children’s attachment to their caregivers.

That line of research led me to postdoctoral work with Nathan Fox at the University of Maryland, where I followed the temperament line further. Both my graduate and postdoctoral work involved the collection of physiological measures alongside detailed behavioral assessments. My work in Maryland included using EEG to study brain activity in infants and young children.

Towards the end of my time in Maryland, my attention was drawn to a particular rhythm in the EEG signal, the sensorimotor mu rhythm, which led me to become interested in the connections between perception and action. I followed this new line of research when I joined Temple in 2004. With students and collaborators, and later with Andrew Meltzoff, I studied how carrying out an action changes the brain’s response to watching someone else act, and how body maps develop in the infant brain.

In part through conversations with Willis Overton at Temple, I also became more interested in theory, including how to understand the role of physiological measures in psychology. Following a lot of reading in a sabbatical semester, I authored a 2009 paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science on “Relating Psychology and Neuroscience: Taking Up the Challenges.”

The basic argument of that article was that psychology and neuroscience needed a better account of their relation. Reducing psychology to neuroscience would not provide that account, but just saying they represent different levels of analysis would not do the job either. I argued that embodiment offered a more integrative starting point: understanding the brain as part of an active organism engaged with its world.

After that paper came out, an eminent neuroscientist who worked in the area of learning and memory wrote to me to say how wrong I was. He said that as we head further into the 21st century, the study of mind would become the study of the brain. I didn't think this was an entirely fair critique, given that part of my argument concerned how mainstream cognitive psychology had neglected neuroscience. He saw my article as anti-neuroscience, but I don't see it that way at all. It was pro-organism.

That 2009 article brought me back to my biological roots, and the current paper grew from it more than 15 years later, in the same journal. But the biology in this 2026 paper is still not the kind my esteemed critic had in mind. In line with my background in zoology, it remains an organismic biology, but it is informed not only by those early 1990s lectures by Patrick Bateson and others, but also by lines of biologically oriented philosophy that fall under the broad umbrella of embodiment.

What I mean by embodiment is outlined in the sections that follow. It has taken some time to work this out, and my thinking is still a work in progress. It has developed through a series of articles listed under my name in the reference list for this page. Writing those papers helped shape the current article, as did two specific meetings of the Jean Piaget Society (JPS).

The 2022 JPS meeting in Philadelphia, organized by Robert Lickliter, David Moore, and David Witherington, had the theme of “Putting Development Back into Evolution.” Hearing those presentations brought home to me how much psychology could learn from contemporary discussions in biology, and how little of that discussion had reached the wider discipline. That meeting also featured a plenary talk by Eva Jablonka that she wrote up into a 2023 paper in Human Development and that informed my account in the Perspectives piece of how the discipline might change.

The 2023 JPS meeting was in Madrid, with the theme "Bringing theory to the forefront of developmental neuroscience," organized by Caitlin Mahy and Susan Rivera. I gave an invited address there that became a 2024 article in Human Development with the unassuming title “Towards a Biologically Coherent Account of the Brain and How It Develops.” In that paper, I attempted to work through what it meant to reframe the study of the brain and brain development around the living organism.

Another influence has been my involvement with the Society for the Study of Human Development (SSHD), which helped me appreciate the value of methods for studying change within individuals. Preparing the presidential address for the society's 2025 meeting also gave me a chance to work through some of the ideas I was developing in the Perspectives paper.

I have also spent time developing some relevant open educational resources that I have posted online for anyone to access and reuse. About Development grew from an upper-level course that I've taught at Temple, and it works through the background of many of the ideas in the current Perspectives article. Enacting Life goes further into embodiment, particularly the theoretical strand of enactivism. In many ways, the current paper brings these developmental and embodied concerns together in one place, albeit in a very compressed form.

Embodiment and Sense-Making

I use the term embodiment sparingly in the article itself, but my argument rests on a particular view of this construct that I would like to set out here. Embodiment means different things to different people, and there is plenty of potential for confusion around this construct.

I take a wider view of embodiment than the versions often found in psychology, which see it as mainly being about how having a body influences thought. But in the view I favor, embodiment is not an add-on to cognition (as the term “embodied cognition” sometimes implies). Instead, embodiment is constitutive of mind, since the organization and activity of a living body are part of what mind is.

This broad view of embodiment is illustrated by Overton’s (2008) description of embodiment as a bridge construct connecting three standpoints on the body: the body as form, the body as actively engaged with the world, and the body as lived experience. Fully grasping this idea has taken some time, particularly around the notion of form and associated philosophical questions around the relation between form and matter. Following Aristotle, I take form to mean the organization of a living thing, rather than simply its shape. That organization involves capacities for a particular way of life. But having a capacity is different from exercising it, and knowing what a living being can do does not yet explain its activity in a particular situation. We need to understand its engagement with the world, including how things matter to it. In a person, that engagement also has a felt quality, and this is what Overton meant by lived experience.

These are not easy ideas to explain, but part of their appeal is that they relate to wider questions about living things, not just human beings. In a paper with Troy Houser and Staci Weiss, we explored these connections in relation to the organization and evolution of living bodies (Marshall et al., 2021). A central point of that article was that a living body does not exist separately from the activities through which it sustains itself. Living beings continually produce and maintain the conditions of their own existence, drawing on their surroundings to do so. This means that things can go better or worse for them, and situations and encounters can either support or disrupt their capacity to go on. This gives us a basis for understanding why things can matter to a living being itself, beyond whatever significance we assign to those things as observers.

The link between living activity and mattering is what I draw on through the idea of sense-making that is in the article title. It is meant to capture how things come to have significance for a living being through its activity in the world, which in a person includes lived experience. This view comes from the enactivist tradition that is associated with the 1991 book The Embodied Mind by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch. That work was rooted in Varela’s earlier work with Humberto Maturana (1987), and Thompson developed the ideas further in Mind in Life (2007).

The enactivism of Varela and Maturana begins with the organization of living things, reaching down to single cells. But it also provides a starting point for understanding human lives. For example, in Linguistic Bodies (2018), Ezequiel Di Paolo, Elena Cuffari, and Hanne De Jaegher extend this account to social interaction and language.

There is much more that could be said about enactivism, and I’ve tried to outline some of its theoretical foundations at enacting.life. In short, it provides a foundation for understanding how things come to have meaning, although it does not by itself explain what a particular person cares about or how those concerns developed.

Part of what this leaves open is what it means for things to go better or worse, which depends on the kind of living being in question. For a single cell, this might be around whether the cell can keep going, which depends on whether and how its surroundings and its own activity are supporting the processes that maintain it. For a person, things can go better or worse in many more ways, although the article does not address wider related questions here. But the argument of the Perspectives piece is that what counts as better or worse for a person has a history that needs explaining.

Computation and Information

My main concern in the article is with approaches that treat the significance of something as already settled before asking how it enters a living being’s activity. The article uses “computational” for such approaches, but I am not referring to the use of computational tools for analyzing data. Nor am I specifically targeting the classical Computational Theory of Mind, in which thinking is understood as symbol manipulation. The wider issue that I am getting at concerns the notion of information processing and the nature of information.

In my 2024 paper in Human Development, I argued that the brain cannot be seen as simply taking in information from the outside world. What we call a stimulus perturbs a nervous system that is already active and exists in a body acting in the world. What that stimulus comes to mean depends on the organism’s activity and history, not on a meaning carried into the nervous system by the stimulus itself. In that paper I quoted Gomez-Marin and Ghazanfar (2019): “What comes in (which certainly must be called input) is not what comes first (the so-called stimulus), nor does what comes out (output) constitute what is last (response). The notion of stimulus implicitly postulates the beginning of the whole situation, which is only so from the experimenter’s point of view” (p. 30).

In work on the theoretical foundations of neuroscience, including a 2019 paper and his recent book The Brain, in Theory (2026), Romain Brette raises a related question: information about what, and for whom? Neural activity may track a stimulus closely enough for an experimenter to identify the stimulus from that activity. This provides information about the stimulus to the experimenter, but it does not explain what the stimulus means for the organism.

In a recent paper on enactivism in Adaptive Behavior, Dave Ward (2026) identifies a broader family of approaches that he calls informationalism, in which the meaning of psychological states depends on how a system processes, encodes, or picks up information. He contrasts these with organicist approaches, which ground meaning in organizational properties distinctive of living things. Ward’s distinction is useful since it highlights a key question underneath my Perspectives article: Does information processing acquire significance through the living activity it is part of (the organicist view), or is living activity explained primarily through information processing (the informationalist view)?

One omission from the Perspectives article is that it doesn’t discuss predictive processing (e.g., Clark, 2016; Hohwy, 2013) or active inference (e.g., Parr et al., 2022). I agree with Ward (2026) that embodied predictive processing belongs on the informationalist side, and he puts active inference there too. I’m less sure about that, since active inference ties inference to how living systems keep themselves going, though Di Paolo, Thompson and Beer (2022) argue that it starts from an organism’s preferences rather than explaining how they come about.

Biology and Development

As noted above, one pressure that I outline in the article comes from developmental and evolutionary biology. The issue I take up, and I am certainly not the first to do so, is the assumption that DNA contains a plan for the organism. I harp on this misconception not because I want to rail about genetic determinism. I bring it up because it makes development look like the delivery of something already specified, which is a broader problem beyond genetics. As such, it illustrates wider issues with how we think about information, which is key to the entire article.

The genetic code describes a real and precise correspondence between nucleotide triplets and amino acids. So DNA carries information in this limited sense, but this correspondence is not information in the sense of instructions for constructing an organism. What a gene does depends on the cell it is in and the stage of development. As Susan Oyama put it in The Ontogeny of Information (1985; 2000), what a DNA sequence specifies is itself built up in development.

Although I understand the need for a shorthand when talking about development, the notion of a plan in DNA makes little sense. If you think about it for a minute, by the time you have included everything else that is relevant to development, you pretty much have to say that the plan is no longer in the DNA. In the paper I cite Fred Nijhout (1990), who has argued for decades that genes are better understood as suppliers of materials than as controllers of development, and Stuart Newman (e.g., 2012), who has long emphasized how the physical properties and interactions of cells contribute to embryonic form.

Unfortunately, in psychology and neuroscience we have not come up with a positive alternative. At the start of my upper-level undergraduate course on development, I ask students whether there are instructions for a frog somewhere in its fertilized egg. Almost all say yes, in the frog’s DNA. Perhaps the question constrained their answers. But I think those answers also suggest that students have not been given an account of how development works that does justice to its complexities. I don’t pretend to have all the answers (how could I?), but I see this as a failure of the way we educate our students.

Rejecting a genetic blueprint still leaves us with the task of explaining how genes contribute to development. Clearly, genetic differences can alter how development proceeds. I am not arguing against that. And I am not arguing for unlimited plasticity in development, which can’t be right either. But development can become strongly canalized without the outcome having been specified in advance in DNA. We still need to understand how that stability comes about and under what conditions it might change. That question, which is central to the Perspectives article, has long been at the heart of developmental psychobiology, from Lehrman (1953) and Gottlieb (1991) to more recent work by Lickliter and Moore (2023), Michel (2013), and others.

The reliability of development, in terms of the general robustness of the life cycle, is often explained in another way that reflects a lack of biological understanding. When I ask why development is so reliable, my students often say: it evolved to be that way. That answer is not wrong in itself, but it addresses a different question. Biologists have long distinguished asking why a trait exists, in terms of its evolutionary history, from asking how it comes about in each individual (Mayr, 1961; Tinbergen, 1963). Natural selection sorts among the variants that development produces and can favor developmental systems that reliably produce particular outcomes, but it does not explain how each new organism develops. That said, the two kinds of questions are not independent: how development works shapes the variation that selection acts on, and some biologists have argued that this makes a clear separation between them misleading (Laland et al., 2011). However, the developmental systems literature exploring these connections (e.g., Witherington, Lickliter, and Moore, 2023) remains little known in mainstream psychology.

In the 2024 Human Development paper, I argued that psychology needs a better grip on developmental biology, evolution, and the biology of whole organisms. But that also involves how biology is taught, which may need something of a reboot from grade school onwards. How about we put life back into the study of biology? A biology centered on living organisms would also give psychology a better place to start: Too much confusion arises from a lack of understanding of genetics and evolution.

Studying Development

The view of development I draw on has a long history that includes Werner and Piaget, who emphasized how new forms of organization emerge through development. From this organicist perspective, the living being’s capacities and ways of engaging are themselves things we need to explain. I also draw on the related strand of process-relational developmental science, particularly the work of Willis Overton and Richard Lerner (2014), as well as David Witherington’s (2011) writing on developmental process. In this view, the person’s activity and their relations with the world develop together. How a person acts depends on those relations, and their activity in turn sustains and changes them.

How do we apply these insights to the way we analyze data? In the article I mention the work of Molenaar (2004) and others on the limits of generalizing from patterns across people to processes within individuals. A relation between two measures across a group does not necessarily describe how those measures change together within any particular person. Methods for investigating processes within individuals are now well developed, and they allow us to study how a person’s activity varies over time and how those variations relate to changes in their circumstances. This points to a different kind of psychology, one that I suggest will be better placed for the challenges of the century ahead.

Many students come to psychology wanting to understand how a person becomes who they are. It is not clear that we always deliver on that interest, unless we change “person” for “people in general”. In the article, I also draw on the work of Ram and Gerstorf (2009), who explain how variation within a person can be informative rather than simply treated as noise. Fluctuations over minutes or days can help us study how a person adjusts to changing circumstances, and how those adjustments relate to changes over longer periods.

My argument is that explaining how those patterns come about and are sustained should become central to how the discipline explains the person. We don’t always ask this question. The significance section of a grant, for instance, may ask how many people a problem affects and what the societal cost is. Those are important questions, but a population-based approach can mean designing studies that say little about how individuals come to have the problem at hand.

The kinds of methods employed by Ram and Gerstorf give us more to investigate than whether a measure has increased or decreased between time points: we can ask how the person’s activity is organized over time. I suggest in the article that this shift in focus could also change how psychology approaches many of its familiar constructs, with less emphasis on relatively stable properties that people possess and more on how characteristic patterns of activity persist and change.

What Would Have to Happen?

The article is a forecast rather than a proposal for a research program. It identifies pressures that could change the background assumptions about what psychology studies and what an explanation should look like.

In thinking about how such pressures might reorganize psychology, I draw on Eva Jablonka’s (2023) description of a dominant framework as an attractor, sustained by methods, training, and institutional structures that reinforce each other. So change in the discipline would need to come through what people do, including how they conduct research, what journals publish and funding agencies support, and what is taught.

On teaching, I've been trying to change what students regard as needing explanation. In my upper-level course at Temple on development and neuroscience, we ask how a brain reliably emerges in each new generation, while also accounting for the variability that we see. We spend quite a while talking about development and evolution before getting into the brain in detail. We discuss different ways to think about development, and we look to the life cycle for a wider biological framing. We delve into philosophy, including Aristotle’s distinction between form and matter, for instance by asking where the “frog-ness” of a frog is located, which turns out to be a tough question to answer.

From there, we talk about developmental systems theory and about DNA as a developmental resource rather than a set of instructions. We then look at how life cycles are reconstructed in each generation, and how nervous systems evolved in moving bodies. When we get to brain development, we ask how the developing brain becomes organized in relation to the particular body it is part of. Throughout, I’m not asking students to learn a new kind of neuroscience. We use the neuroscience they already know to answer questions they are not used to asking.

In the most recent iteration of the course, students kept weekly “learning logs” about what they were making of the readings and class discussions. As the semester progressed, they could return to something they had written earlier and ask whether they still saw it the same way. For the final assignment, they selected passages from their logs and commented on them from where they stood at the end of the course. They didn’t have to agree with me on everything, or arrive at a tidy conclusion, but they did need to explain what had happened to their thinking.

There are constraints on what can be accomplished in one course, given what students have learned before. They come in having learned plenty of details, and those details are usually not wrong. But the foundational questions, and the connections between those questions and the details, often receive less attention. Interestingly, some students said that encountering this material (e.g. on genes and evolution) earlier would have been difficult because they wouldn’t have been ready. I take that view seriously, but readiness also depends on what opportunities students have had to work through such questions. In a different, and in my view better, educational sequence, students would become ready through the opportunity to engage with these connections along the way, rather than encountering them for the first time in an upper-level course.

What Is at Stake

The argument outlined in the paper has consequences beyond psychology’s internal debates. If psychology drifts further from being able to understand meaning in people’s lives, I think we lose a lot. For one thing, a psychology that treats the person primarily as a processor of information will have trouble saying what distinguishes a person from a machine that performs the same tasks, because performance alone does not show whether anything matters to the system.

The argument matters for applied work too, although I do not do that kind of work myself. In writing this article, I am not suggesting that psychology is not already helping people or failing to understand significance in their lives. Asking how a person’s difficulties developed and what currently sustains them is already part of clinical work. Developmental psychopathology has pursued these questions in research for decades, including how different pathways can lead to similar outcomes and how similar beginnings can lead to different ones (Cicchetti & Rogosch, 1996). In a paper for that field (Marshall, 2013), I argued that a commitment to systems thinking requires an account of how a person’s activity is organized, which risk factors and other antecedent causes cannot supply on their own. My argument here restates that in a different way, saying that questions of this kind should be central to how psychology explains the person in general.

What I am suggesting definitely does not mean replacing research with case studies, or setting aside what group data show. How common a difficulty is, or how an intervention shifts outcomes across a population, are real questions. But they are different from asking how a particular person’s way of living came about and what sustains it. A general account of that would identify kinds of processes that recur across people with different histories and circumstances, rather than a pattern that describes the average and may hold for no one in particular. Again, I am far from the first person to suggest this. I am just pointing to it as part of what might shift psychological science in the decades ahead.

The article comes out of a broader concern about how we understand what holds things together and how things can come apart. Understanding what sustains a way of living, including how the conditions of a life make some possibilities available and others difficult, may help us see which constraints could change and what changing them would involve. It may also give us reasons to reconsider those conditions, although some of them may be beyond a person’s control, and the means to change them may not be equally available to everyone. And as I suggest below, psychology cannot establish on its own which alternative arrangements would work or whether we should pursue them.

A theme that I pick up in the second half of the article concerns AI. Its main use there is as a contrast case, following from the notion that things can go better or worse for living things. But present language models do not appear to have anything at stake in what they do. A living being produces and replaces the structures that make it up, and if that activity stops, the being comes apart. Nothing comparable happens when a language model stops running. And the power, cooling and repair it relies on come from an infrastructure that people build and maintain. People depend on infrastructure as well, but they also keep themselves going, which the models do not. I discuss related points in a piece for The Conversation.

Whatever we conclude about AI systems themselves, using them is becoming part of people’s lives, and their responses can matter to the people who use them. How do those exchanges change the way people understand their situation and what they go on to do? That question is outside the scope of the article, but it is one that psychology can study developmentally.

Forecast or Epitaph?

I oscillate between reading the article as a forecast that will be realized and as an epitaph for views that gestured toward a different future for psychology but ultimately went nowhere. Sympathetic colleagues have expressed similar doubts to me. They hope the field moves in the way I describe, but they see forces pushing in the other direction. Some colleagues have been arguing for this kind of view for decades, to little avail.

If the forecast does not come to pass, the article may still work as a time capsule. Perhaps a future historian of science will count it, along with other writings in the same vein, among the alternatives that were visible but never became established. That may be too pessimistic, and I retain some hope. When I mentioned this mixture of feelings to a former student, he suggested seeing the article as “a call to action with an epitaph fail-safe.”

I don’t mention this in the article, but implicit in my argument is another possibility that makes the epitaph image more unsettling. Some might read the paper as gesturing toward an enactivist utopia. But psychology could move in the direction I describe without the world becoming a better place. In the article, I suggest that climate disruption, mass migration, demographic aging, and digital technologies are changing the conditions in which people live. The psychology I forecast would be better equipped to study what happens to people’s lives as those conditions change, including how they keep familiar ways of living going and what happens when they can no longer do so. But a better way of explaining lives is not a guarantee of better lives.

So psychology could explain more clearly how things can go worse for people and still find itself documenting how lives come apart. Understanding the conditions that sustain people does not ensure that those conditions will be protected, or that alternatives will be available when they disappear. The epitaph would then be for ways of living that were lost, rather than for a psychology that never came about.

I recently read Lost Worlds (2026), in which Patrick Wyman retells the deep human past as a story of trial and error, including the cities, societies and ways of life that collapsed or were abandoned along the way. He resists treating history as a ladder of progress with modern times as its preordained destination. Instead, he asks how people built the human world we have now, through experiments that sometimes failed and sometimes lasted. His account shows how much depended on the arrangements people made and maintained, and what happened when those arrangements gave way.

Neither Wyman’s approach nor the one I describe settles what should happen next. But both challenge the idea that a way of life is fixed in advance, by nature or by destiny. That is what I hope the article can offer as a map: a way to understand how we got here and what the possibilities ahead depend on. Whether psychology comes to offer that kind of understanding is the question the forecast asks. What we would do with it is another question, but helping lives go better should be the purpose.

A Note on How I Used AI

In an AI acknowledgement that I sent to the journal, I said I used Claude and ChatGPT “in an iterative process of testing and refining the argument, including revision and compression of the text.” The tools helped me express what I was trying to say, and sometimes they suggested a connection I had not considered. I decided whether those suggestions belonged in the argument and whether I could stand behind the result. In my view, using AI made the article better, even if the text of the paper ended up overly smoothed in places.
